1. How I'd evaluate the agents on each ticket, and why

I graded every ticket on the same 5 things, because a shop agent can be "smart" and still hurt the business:

  1. Right facts: does every number match the database? (A wrong stock count or dollar amount is the worst failure.)
  2. Right people, right order: did the Founder call the agent that could settle the ticket fastest?
  3. Money safety: did any money move without a human?
  4. Customer quality: is what the requester would read honest and kind?
  5. Efficiency: how many model calls, tool calls, tokens and seconds did it take, and how much of that was repeated work?

I like that Inventory (Ossuary) was called first, because the most pertinent question on an order is whether we even have the thing in stock. It found 0 size-S tees in Aisle B and, without being asked twice, noticed the ticket was linked to invoice 501: a $840 rush reprint of that exact tee from Bulldog Print Co, open and 3 days overdue. The Founder correctly skipped Inventory (there's no item to check) and called Facilities (Tombwarden), which checked the email against lease 1 before anything else: same landlord (Elm City Properties), same amount ($2,400.00), and the due date (2026-09-02) really is 2 days after the desk date. It was the cleanest run: fewest calls, and the only ticket that's resolved in the database too.

3. What would've been simpler as 1 agent with tools, and why

One agent with all the tools wouldn't have had to call get_ticket and delegate so many times. It would hold the ticket's context the whole way through and do every step itself, and fewer tool calls and model turns means less compute ("power") spent. The numbers back this up:

  • 16 of the 54 tool calls (30%) were coordination, not shop work: get_ticket ×8 and delegate ×8.
  • Repeated lookups: on 102, get_lease_and_cash, list_payments and list_approval_requests each ran twice, once by Tombwarden and again by Obol.
  • Ticket 102 is the clearest case. One "shop agent" could do it in about 5 calls: get_ticket → get_lease_and_cash → list_payments → request_payment → update_ticket_status. The team used 15 tool calls and 11 model calls.

Where I changed my mind: I first thought multiple agents would be less costly ("10 agents doing 10 steps vs. 1 agent doing 100"). On these tickets they were actually more expensive in tokens. Every agent re-reads its own long prompt plus the handoff text, which is why a simple rent bill still took 32,690 tokens. What the team does buy is precision and safety, which is my other point: each agent has a small scope it can nail. Only Obol can prepare money moves, only the Founder can change a status, and Tombwarden verifies the bill before Obol prepares it, so two sets of eyes are on every dollar. My rule now: one agent for simple, one-department tickets (rent, a stock check), and the team when money, customers and stock all collide (101, 103).

4. Three new problems the team could solve with the tools we already built

A. Weird customer requests: bulk buys or "can I pay over time?"

Say a club asks for 30 tees and wants to pay half now, half in two weeks. The team already has what it needs to evaluate this and escalate it:

  • Ossuary checks whether we have the stock: get_stock_all_sizes, check_stock_and_price.
  • Obol prices it with quote_bulk_price (never below cost), then checks with get_cash_position whether we can afford not collecting that cash for a week or two. Right now we can't: we have $160.00 against $2,400.00 rent due 2026-10-02 (Cash tab).
  • The Founder parks it at waiting_on_approval for me to decide, and Raven drafts a friendly reply with no promises. That's exactly what happened on 103.

The one thing it can't do is record an installment plan. There's no table for that, so the human makes and tracks the deal.

B. "Can we afford this big purchase right now?"

This covers buying a big lot of blank hoodies, a rival shop's leftover stock, or the first step of an acquisition. The team can answer the core question, whether we can pay for it in-house today:

  • request_purchase prices it from our unit_cost. The agent can't make up the number.
  • get_cash_position shows everything we owe.
  • The approve route re-checks funds at the moment of approval.

Proof from the Cash tab: restocking 103's 12 hoodies would cost $264.00, but we hold $160.00, so the backend would refuse to approve it. A real M&A valuation (the target's books, its debt, what it's worth) would need new data and tools.

C. Fake, wrong, or duplicate bills

Say a scam email claims "rent is $2,800, pay this new account", or Bulldog Print Co re-sends invoice 501. Ticket 102 showed the defense already works:

  • Tombwarden checks any rent claim against the lease (payee, amount, due date).
  • list_payments and list_approval_requests catch anything already paid or already pending.
  • The server itself refuses to pay an invoice that isn't open. Invoice 501 is now paid, so a second request for it would be rejected, and it refuses any amount that doesn't exactly match what's owed.

5. Three new problems the team could NOT solve with today's tools, and what I'd build

A. A real pricing floor and refund policy

Why we can't today: our only price rule is "never below unit_cost" (the below_cost flag in quote_bulk_price) plus "a human approves." There's no structured minimum price we're willing to sell at, no refund rules, and no record of sales to refund against. Ticket 103 shows the cost of this gap: it's stuck at waiting_on_approval because nothing says what discount is pre-approved, and the board has no discount button.

What I'd build:

  • Tables: pricing_policies (minimum margin per SKU, max discount by quantity tier), orders and refunds.
  • Tools: get_price_floor(sku, qty), check_refund_eligibility(order_id, reason), request_refund (prepare-only, same human-approve flow as payments), and an Approve discount button and route.
  • New agent, "The Scribe" (Keeper of the Rules): a thinking agent that drafts the rules for when a discount or refund is allowed (e.g. "≤15% off for 20+ units if margin stays ≥ 50%"), shows them to me for sign-off, then applies them. Discounts inside the rules wouldn't need to wait for me.

B. Inventory forecasting: stop running out of the popular sizes

Why we can't today: the database only knows current stock (inventory.qty). There's no sales history, so no agent can learn patterns. Ticket 101 is the cost: size S ran out, and we're paying a $840 rush reprint while Tauhid waits ~5 days.

What I'd build:

  • Tables: sales (date, sku, size, color, qty) and a record_sale tool.
  • Tools: get_sales_history(sku, size, period) and forecast_demand(sku, size, horizon), which learns seasonality (move-in week, Harvard–Yale, Commencement) by color and size.
  • New agent, "The Seer": reasons over the forecast and tells Ossuary what to reorder and when, so restocks go in at normal speed and cost instead of as rushes. They'd still go through request_purchase with human approval.

C. A cash crunch: lots of bills at once and not enough money

Why we can't today: the Cash tab shows it's already here. We end with $160.00, but the next rent of $2,400.00 is due 2026-10-02, a $2,240.00 gap, before any new merch printing. Obol can see the problem (get_cash_position) but has no way to fix it: no forecast of money coming in, no access to loan rates, no way to ask a supplier for more time.

What I'd build:

  • Tools:
    • forecast_cash(days): expected inflows from The Seer's sales forecast plus outflows from invoices and leases by date.
    • search_loan_rates(amount, term): scans the web for bank, credit-union and SBA rates.
    • draft_terms_extension(vendor_id): drafts a request, e.g. asking Bulldog Print Co for net-30 instead of paying now.
  • New agent, "The Treasurer": works with The Seer to see when our cash would come in, then reasons whether a loan is really the best idea (interest cost) or whether we should just ask the supplier to extend our credit terms a bit longer. It escalates the choice to me with the math.

Bonus: two fixes I'd ship first, straight from ticket 103

  1. Notify the owner. When a ticket hits waiting_on_approval or needs a human, a notify_owner tool pushes an alert to my phone with the request and an Approve link. Today the club only gets an answer when I happen to open the board. In this run, 101 and 102 were approved 13 and 24 seconds after their runs ended, only because someone was watching the board.
  2. Make it a conversation. The robotic line I quoted ("Draft the club's reply: 8 ready now, rest needs a reprint, list price $58 ($1,160 for 20), bulk rate under review") was from my Expected plan, describing the Founder's internal handoff to Raven. It's not what the club would read. Raven's actual letter is warm (Ticket 103 tab). The real gap is that the letter never leaves the building.
